AgentGate.

Context

From copilots that suggest
to agents that act.

Independent concept inspired by DTCC's public AI journey. Not affiliated with or endorsed by DTCC. Firms and the trade in the demo are fictional.

DTCC's public journey gave me a concrete starting point. AWS reports that its 2024 Amazon Q Developer proof of concept increased developer throughput by 40% and reduced code defects by 30%. The rollout sat within an AI policy, a central inventory of initiatives, and a cross-functional oversight group reporting monthly. AWS case study.

In January 2026, DTCC's CIO described the firm beginning to use AI agents and coding tools. That prompted my product question: when software can take the next action, how should authority move through the workflow? DTCC leadership outlook.

AgentGate is my exploration of that question, not an assessment of DTCC's internal controls. It shows how a market infrastructure firm could move from approving AI projects to governing individual AI actions.

01 · Decode the Business Problem

Agents need controls
at the moment of action.

Copilots suggest; agents act. In the assisted coding workflow I used as a starting point, an engineer could review a suggestion before accepting it. The human remained the safety check.

For any firm, a project-level review cannot by itself intercept a wrong message at 9:06 on a Tuesday. An agent's permissions need to be checked when it takes the action.

The business problem: what may an agent do alone, and who notices when it gets something wrong?

I focused on the permission boundary: make useful investigation autonomous, make consequential authority explicit, and leave an understandable trail of what happened.

02 · Map the User Workflow

Learn the domain.
Then map the analyst's day.

A settlement fail means the agreed exchange of shares and cash did not complete by the deadline. Most US equities operate on a T+1 settlement cycle. I researched the analyst's process before deciding where an agent belonged.

The cause categories I designed around were a seller short of securities, mismatched settlement instructions, and insufficient cash or collateral. These are workflow scenarios, not a frequency ranking inferred from the SEC file.

  1. Find the fail.
  2. Classify the fail type.
  3. Research the cause.
  4. Chase the counterparty.
  5. Fix the issue.
  6. Monitor until settlement.
  7. Close and document.

Honest starting point: I did not know settlements before this project. I learned the domain by building it, using Perplexity for research and the SEC's raw data for evidence.

03 · Find the AI Lever

The lever is not autonomy.
It is the permission policy.

I mapped permissions to actions rather than treating the agent as either trusted or untrusted. Green means the agent acts alone. Amber requires human approval. Red reserves the action for a human.

StepPermission
Find the failGreen Agent acts alone.
Classify the fail typeGreen Agent acts alone.

Close and document: unresolvedRed Closure blocked if anything remains open or settlement is unconfirmed.

Key design idea: conditional permissions. The same action gets a different permission level depending on the evidence the agent has. Matching a standing instruction is different from guessing what the instruction should be.

04 · Prototype the Decision

Three facts.
One human decision.

In the fictional scenario, Northbridge Capital buys 10,000 Alphabet Class A (GOOGL) shares from Halden Securities. Settlement fails because the broker is short of shares.

The agent detects, classifies, and researches the fail alone. It then stops: the fix requires borrowing shares, which is human-only. At the 09:06 escalation, the analyst gets three decision facts:

2h 54m
Time to the 12:00 deadline
2h 24m
Expected incoming shares; 30-minute buffer
8,000
Shares available now out of 10,000
Borrow
The analyst arranges the borrow. The agent monitors receipt and confirmation before closing and documenting the case.
Wait
The analyst accepts the timing risk. The agent monitors the expected incoming shares, with only a 30-minute buffer.
Partial settlement
Settle the 8,000 available shares, leaving 2,000 open. The agent cannot close the case until the remaining shares settle.
The close rule does real work
Choosing a path is not the same as resolving the exception. Closure depends on confirmed settlement, not a confident narrative.

Human authority. Agent accountability. The prototype makes the stop visible, gives the analyst enough context to decide, and follows a different governed path for each choice.

How to test this in 60 seconds
The Guided demo tests scripted governance logic, not live AI performance. Reset between checks: choose Partial and confirm 2,000 shares remain open; choose Borrow and confirm settlement precedes closure; inspect the initial timeline and confirm the agent pauses at the red authority boundary. The initial red entry describes a blocked action, not a completed execution.

Live AI extension: the AI proposes; the policy decides. Case library analyzes fictional cases through a server-only AI gateway. A separate TypeScript policy assigns permissions. Evaluate checks eight synthetic cases against known answers and calculates results for that run. Incorrect extracted facts or proposed actions can still produce a wrong permission. All execution is simulated; no trades, transfers, or emails are sent. This small benchmark is not proof of production safety.

Governance rules are covered by automated tests. Run npm test to check the permission, timing, and closure rules.

05 · Measure Adoption and ROI

Ground the problem in data.
Keep outcomes separate from hypotheses.

I analysed the SEC Fails-to-Deliver file for the second half of October 2026: approximately 63,000 rows from NSCC's Continuous Net Settlement system. I ranked the October 30 snapshot by estimated dollar value, using reported fail quantity multiplied by the file's price field, rather than by share count.

The largest dollar balances sat in widely held securities: an S&P 500 ETF, mega-cap stocks, and a leveraged single-stock ETF. Ranking by dollar value does not, by itself, establish concentration or trading activity. GOOGL and the leveraged Google ETF GGLL appearing together raised a research question about related exposures; the snapshot does not establish that one security caused another's fail.

October 30 top-10 analysis extract included: reported quantities, prices, and recomputed dollar estimates. Imported security identifiers are preserved and damaged CUSIPs are flagged.

Reading the data correctly: the SEC reports aggregate outstanding fail balances, not new failures per day. Its price field generally reflects the previous day's closing price. These estimates are context, not a loss calculation, and the file does not identify the cause of each fail. SEC dataset notes.

The data-quality lesson: importing the file into a spreadsheet silently corrupted security IDs. QQQ's CUSIP, 46090E103, became 4.61E+107, and other identifiers lost leading zeros. I would import CUSIPs as text and validate them before an agent uses them. Data integrity comes before autonomous action.

The metrics below are hypotheses I would track, not measured results from the concept:

  • Time from fail detection to a human decision.
  • Share of agent actions with a defined permission level: target 100% before go-live.
  • Every red-level action logged with a human decision.
  • Time required to produce the monthly AI governance report.
What I'd Build Next

Turn one governed workflow
into a portfolio of governed agents.

1
Independent evidence validation
Validate the model's extracted facts against authoritative settlement records before applying permissions. Expand the synthetic benchmark with practitioner-reviewed edge cases; a human governance lead owns the policy.
2
Duplicate detection
Help teams spot overlapping agents before two groups build the same workflow.
3
An automated monthly portfolio report
Give the AI oversight group a consolidated view of agent activity, permission coverage, escalations, and unresolved cases.

The next validation step is to review the permission map with settlement operations and governance practitioners. The concept demonstrates a product decision; it does not establish production readiness.
